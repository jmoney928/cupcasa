Understanding PLA

A Real Step Forward —
With One Caveat

PLA (polylactic acid) is made from plant starch — typically corn or sugarcane. It's BPA-free, PFAS-free, and a genuine improvement over conventional petroleum plastic. We offer it because it's a meaningful choice for many businesses. Here's what you should know about how it works.

🌱

Plant-Based & BPA-Free

PLA is derived from renewable plant sources, not petroleum. It contains no BPA, PFAS, or phthalates — making it a cleaner choice for customers than conventional plastic cups at every price point.

140°F

Needs Industrial Composting

To fully break down, PLA requires sustained heat above 140°F and controlled humidity — conditions found in industrial composting facilities. Where those facilities exist, PLA breaks down cleanly. Where they don't, it should be directed to recycling, not landfill.

→

The Middle Ground

PLA sits between conventional plastic and fully home-compostable PHA. It's the right choice for businesses who want eco-forward packaging at accessible MOQs, and who are transparent with customers about how to properly dispose of it.

Our commitment: We don't overstate what PLA can do — and we don't understate it either. If industrial composting isn't accessible in your city, PHA is available at MOQ 5,000. If you're starting out, PLA at MOQ 500 is a real improvement over conventional plastic and a solid starting point.

The Solution

What Is PHA?

PHA (Polyhydroxyalkanoates) is a bioplastic produced by bacteria that feed on plant-based sugars and oils. Under nutrient stress, these bacteria store energy by producing PHA granules inside their own cells — essentially growing bioplastic from within a living organism.

The result is a material that behaves like plastic during use, but breaks down in home compost, soil, and seawater after. No industrial infrastructure required. No microplastics. No toxic residue.

Unlike PLA, PHA doesn't need special conditions to decompose. It just breaks down — the way nature intended.

The human body connection: PHAs — including PHB (polyhydroxybutyrate) — are naturally produced in the human body as energy storage molecules. PHA is biocompatible at a molecular level. Your biology already knows what it is, which is why it's used in FDA-approved medical devices, surgical sutures, and drug delivery applications.
